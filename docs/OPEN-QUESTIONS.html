Open questions

Getting started · The game · The tape · The code · Findings

What is still open, keeping MEASURED and ASSUMED apart. What is measured carries its measurement; what is assumed is labelled as such and has not been written into the listing as if it were a fact.

MEASURED

The tape

  • Thirteen blocks. Three named files, all called DEMON: an ASCII one (the BASIC loader), a 249-byte BIN at 0x8180 and a 24,576-byte BIN at 0x8000. Between the two BINs go seven headerless blocks.
  • Line 50 of the BASIC loader is BLOAD"CAS:DEMON",R:RUN. Since tape is sequential, the first pass finds the small BIN and the second — after the RUN — the big one. They share a name on purpose.
  • make verify rebuilds the whole .cas, sha256 69031ee25099df0703332ca74a64b52e6dd9909e4cfded4c3699be153adc5fc9.

The layout in memory

  • The table at 0x8180 declares the seven chunks. tools/cuerpos.py checks that every entry matches the size of its block and aborts if it does not.
  • The five chunks hidden under the ROM match the RAM of a running openMSX byte for byte (tools/coteja_ram.py), and also the RAM snapshots inside a replay recorded on a different machine (a Sony HB-20P). Two independent measurements.
  • The game block differs from the tape by 83 bytes the moment it starts, all within 0x8000-0x80FF. Over 200 seconds of play 134 bytes change, 133 of them on that same page and the other one being 0x8C5E.

Geometries

  • Sprites: 33-byte records (32 of 16×16 pattern + 1 of colour), in two banks, 242 records at 0x0000 and 214 at 0x1F32. It comes from the routine at 0x8292 and both counts come out exact.
  • Screen maps: 26 maps compressed with 0xFF <tile> <how many>. Decompressed they give 640 tiles (32×20) each and end exactly where the next begins. The decompressor is 0x819D.
  • Scenery: 2,048 bytes of pattern at 0x6000 and 2,048 of colour at 0x6800, uploaded to all THREE thirds of the screen (0x8150). The three thirds share their artwork.
  • Music: four-byte steps (coarse and fine tone for channel B, and for channel C). The pointer starts at 0x7900 and on reaching 0xFF rewinds to 0x79C8, not to the beginning.

The title

The game is called DEMONIA, and its own title screen says so: the compressed map at 0x4281, decompressed and drawn with its patterns (tools/mapas.py, PNG in docs/imagenes/portada-titulo.png), carries DEMONIA in huge letters across the first eight rows. Underneath, in small letters, L'INTRUSION on row 17 and (C) 1986 PAR SABLATOU CLAUDE on row 19.

Those two bottom rows are the only ones that read back if the map is dumped as TEXT, because the title is made of graphic tiles and not of ASCII. A dump like that fooled us once, and that is where the idea that the game was called L'INTRUSION came from: it is the subtitle. The tape is called DEMONIA too.

ASSUMED (labelled as such, and not written into the listing as fact)

  • That 0x9C72 is a debugging back door. What is measured is that it reads INTFLG (0xFC9B), compares against 3 and calls the monitor's entry point. That the intent was to leave a door open is a reading, not a datum.
  • That the ld hl,(0x8078) in the single-step buffer at 0xCC5F is the fossil of a debugging session on this game. What is measured are the four bytes and that 0x8078 falls in the variable page. It fits, but the binary does not say so.
  • That the monitor's command table was erased by the author himself when he built the game on top. What is measured is that there are screen records at 0xD5C6 and not a table. Who did it and when, no.
  • The loader's two initial values (0x81F8 = 0x8188, 0x81FA = 0xC050) are consistent with a photograph of memory taken while chunk 2 was running. That is all that can be said: the code writes both before reading them, so they do not matter.

OPEN: what could not be closed

1. The user's replay cannot be reproduced here

Demonia.omr was recorded on a Sony HB-20P and openMSX asks for its ROM by sha1 (63050d2d21214a721cc55f152c22b7be8061ac33). That ROM is not on this machine and cannot be downloaded.

Three attempts were made:

  1. Repointing the tape reference to the local copy with tools/prepara_replay.py. The path is corrected (24 references), but the failure was elsewhere.
  2. Putting a substitute MSX1 ROM under that name into a private filepool, without touching the user's installation. openMSX resolves system ROMs by sha1, not by name, and rejects it.
  3. Rewriting the sha1 inside the .omr to point at the substitute. The replay loads and runs its 926 seconds, but with a different ROM the tape load desynchronises: 100% of the 1,854,327 PC samples land in the BIOS cassette routines (0x046F, 0x047B, 0x1B2A…) and the game never gets going. The measurement is worthless and has not been used.

What the replay did give up, without an emulator: opening it with gzip and decompressing its twelve RAM snapshots. From the fourth on (t=256 s) the game block matches the tape 99.5%, and the five chunks hidden under the ROM match 100%. So in a real game the memory layout is exactly the one this project describes.

To close it you need: the file hb-20p_basic-bios1.rom with that sha1 in openMSX's systemroms. With that, tools/omsx_replay.tcl gives the PC sampling for a whole game and section 6 of Findings stops being a weak datum.

2. What the game does, played (half closed)

It has now been played, and it is written up in The game: a session in openMSX on a Philips VG-8020 with the real tape. From it come the measured controls (cursor keys through GTSTCK, space bar through GTTRIG), that the player really moves — its X, at 0x8049, runs from 128 up to 152 and back — the panel with SCORE=, RECORD= and the FORCE= bar, and the check that is worth most: the first screen the emulator paints is identical to the map tools/mapas.py draws from the bytes at 0x43DE, with no emulator involved.

What is still open is the WHOLE game: visiting all 24 screens, seeing the character die and reaching the GAME OVER, and tying each screen to its 88-byte record and its four behaviour routines. Synthesised keystrokes are good for walking about for a while, not for playing a game through; for that the right road is still the replay in point 1.

3. The screen records, inside

We know they are 24 records of 88 bytes at 0xD0C0-0xD8FF and that byte 84 of each carries the marker FF <screen number in ASCII> FF. The remaining 88 bytes have not been broken down field by field. There are visibly groups of three bytes starting with 0x06, 0x0E, 0x15 and 0x1B — numbers that grow and that could be positions — but that is an impression, not a measurement, and so it is not in the listing.

4. The short unidentified tables

Twelve data ranges are left with a name but no explained use, because we know WHO reads them but not WHAT FOR: 0x89F1, 0x8C58, 0x9B47, 0xA5E4, 0xA7FF, 0xB4F0, 0xB540, 0xBA64, 0xBDB1, 0xDAD7 and the two tile tables 0xA830 and 0xDAA3. In the listing they carry the address of the instruction that loads them, which is what is known.

0xB4F0 we do know the shape of: seven ten-byte records, with fields advancing in a fixed step from one record to the next (+0x18 in the third byte, +0x300 in the following pair, +1 in the eighth). It looks like a table of seven identical things laid out in a row; what things, has not been measured.

5. The music's tempo

The player at 0x7F90 changes note every 14 calls (0x7F9B). How many calls a second that is has not been measured, so no tempo in notes per minute can be given, nor whether it depends on PAL or NTSC. What is measured are the PSG periods: the first step gives 679 on channel B and 339 on C, that is 329.5 Hz and 659.1 Hz with the MSX clock (3,579,545/16), the same note an octave apart.

6. The three RET NC in the loader

At 0x823A there are three RET NC in a row where one would do: the first has already returned. No explanation has been found. It could be a relative jump that was changed to a RET and left two bytes behind, but that is a guess.

Demonia is a 1986 MSX game; its title screen reads DEMONIA and it is signed (C) 1986 PAR SABLATOU CLAUDE, and the tape is 58,334 bytes. That Microids published it comes from the catalogue, not from the binary. All rights in the game remain with their holders. This is preservation, study and documentation work.
